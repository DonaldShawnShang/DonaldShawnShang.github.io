TL;DR One byte-level Transformer detects attacks across TSN, CAN-over-UDP and SOME/IP traffic, then is distilled ~90× (132M → 1.5M parameters) for in-vehicle deployment.

Coverage: 11 attack types across TSN (AVTP/gPTP), CAN-over-UDP and SOME/IP; 1,474,294 labeled samples in the combined dataset.

🎬How AutoGuard reads traffic

  1. Window
  2. Payload
  3. Tokens
  4. Encoder
  5. Decision

Pick an example window, then press Play, or use Next step to go one stage at a time. A window of w = 5 consecutive packets slides over mixed in-vehicle traffic (stride 1). AutoGuard makes one decision per window. Protocol headers are dropped and each packet keeps its transport-layer payload. The payloads are joined in time order into one byte sequence, capped at L_max bytes. Every two neighbouring bytes become one token: overlapping byte pairs, written as four hex digits. No protocol-specific features are needed. The tokens, wrapped in [CLS] … [SEP], pass through a pre-trained 12-layer Transformer encoder. The output at [CLS] summarises the whole window. A shallow head turns the [CLS] vector into the decision: Normal or Attack, and which of the 11 attack types.

📊Key results

0.9922
F1, binary detection (TOW-IDS)

Accuracy 0.9959 and recall 0.9926 on the public TOW-IDS Automotive Ethernet dataset.

F1 balances precision (how many alerts are real attacks) and recall (how many attacks are caught). 1.0 is perfect.

6.25 ms
Response time per decision

A decision needs only 5 packets: 3.40 ms inference plus 2.85 ms waiting for packets. The compared methods use windows of 45 to 2,048 packets, or 100 ms of traffic.

Response time is measured from packet arrival to a valid decision (buffering + inference), in the paper’s evaluation setup.

90.5×
Smaller student model

Distillation shrinks the model from 132.13M to 1.46M parameters. The cost: binary F1 on the combined dataset drops from 0.9821 to 0.9275.

Parameter count measures model size. The student targets resource-constrained in-vehicle hardware.
